I have lifted weights regularly for five years and am happy to report that being strong(er) has
changed my life for the better. The herniated disc in my lower back now only rarely limits what I
feel comfortable doing, which is a stark improvement compared to five years ago when just sitting
for an hour made it hurt.

Since I progressed to the point that my strength gains are visible to friends and family, several of
them expressed an interest in learning what I do and how I think about strength training. What
follows are my condensed thoughts on the matter.

<aside class="warning">
First, a disclaimer. I'm not a personal trainer. I have no formal education and no training
in sports science, medicine, or anything health related. My first and best piece of advice is to
seek out doctors, personal trainers, and other knowledgeable persons who can give you advice
tailored to your circumstances. But since people persist in asking me...
</aside>

# The Five Laws

---

## 1. Show up

Progress only happens while exercising. You must go to the gym and you must go on a regular
schedule. This is non-negotiable. Do lighter weights if you're tired. Do different exercises if
you're injured. But you must show up. Ultimately, this will depend on your ability to form an
exercise habit. Motivation comes and goes, but routine is forever.

Speaking of injuries...

## 2. No injuries

There is nothing more deleterious to your progress than an extended period of injury-induced
inactivity. Strength training is a marathon, not a sprint; progress is measured in months and years,
not days. And because the human body is calorie-conscious, it will actively destroy muscle that does
not receive regular stimulus. The following is a checklist for avoiding injury:

1. Fix your flexibility. Many people (myself included) start their strength training
journey by looking up workout guides online. These guides almost invariably recommend exercises like
the barbell back squat and overhead press. They also almost invariably omit the fact that you may
not yet be flexible enough to perform these movements safely.

    For example, the barbell back squat requires sufficient ankle flexion to push the knees out over
    the toes, sufficient hip and hamstring flexibility to "sit" while maintaining lumbar lordosis 
    (the natural curve of the lower back), sufficient thoracic spine flexibility to maintain an
    upright torso, and sufficient shoulder mobility to hold the bar against the back. Depending on
    your starting point, it could be months before you're ready to perform the barbell back squat
    with significant amounts of weight.

1. Perform every rep of every set of every exercise with perfect form. No exceptions. If your
form breaks down, you're done. Rest or reduce the weight. At the beginning of your strength training
journey, you may be so weak and/or uncoordinated that you cannot train to muscle failure while
maintaining form. That's to be expected. Focus on form and all else will follow.

1. Never ego lift. Many people (myself included) feel self-conscious when they begin strength
training. They feel embarrassment that they can "only" perform such-and-such exercise with
such-and-such weight. The temptation to increase the weight to an "acceptable" level will be strong.
The temptation to throw on another 20 kilograms to "see what it feels like" will be strong. Ignore
these temptations. Trying to lift too much weight will lead to a breakdown in form, and we already
know that breakdowns in form lead to injury.

    Instead, focus on progressive overload. Add a tiny amount of weight each session, even if it's
    just one extra kilogram. Or do one more rep with the same weight as the previous session. Over
    the course of years, these small increments add up.

1. If you feel pain, stop. Again, strength training is a marathon, not a sprint. Whatever marginal
benefit you might get from one extra rep does not outweigh the risk of injury-induced inactivity.
Some combination of rest, stretching, and rehab with lighter weights is in order.

## 3. Eat right

In response to the stimulus of regular strength training, your body will attempt to build muscle,
strengthen tendons, increase bone density, and more. It will only be able to do those things given
sufficient quantities of raw material. That raw material comes from your diet.

Everyone already knows what a healthy diet looks like: vegetables (more than you eat right now),
fruits, high-quality protein like eggs, fish, and chicken breast (more than you eat right now),
high-quality carbohydrates like oatmeal (but not the stuff with 40 grams of sugar per serving), and
water (more than you drink right now). The hard part is doing, not knowing.

Don't worry, I won't wax polemic against you. Your current eating habits are not your fault. You and
every other person on the planet are hardwired to seek out sugar and fat. And what everyone wants,
capitalism delivers. Our environment is literally engineered to make us unhealthy in the name of
shareholder value.

I still struggle with my diet. It's hard to overcome animal instinct and capitalism. But what I've
found mostly works for me is the following:

1. Eat the same thing for breakfast every day.
1. Rotate between two or three meals for lunch.
1. Rotate between five or six meals for dinner.
1. Eat protein with every single meal. I aim for 40 to 50 grams of protein per meal and supplement
with a protein shake to get approximately 2 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day.
That amount is a good target if you want to maximize muscle protein synthesis, but I acknowledge
that it's an intimidating amount of protein if you're just starting out. Just eating protein with
every meal is a fine place to start.
1. Do not buy snacks unless they are raw fruits or vegetables.

If you do something similar, you'll need to learn to make between eight and ten healthy meals that
you actively enjoy eating. Finding meals you enjoy enough to eat week in and week out will be the
hardest part. Afterward, when you have a set menu, eating right becomes a non-issue.

## 4. Sleep right

Muscle synthesis happens while you sleep. If your sleep is poor, your body won't build muscle AND
you'll have difficulties training to your potential while in the gym.

As with eating right, everyone knows what a healthy sleep schedule looks like. Wake up and go to bed
at the same time every day. Aim for ~eight hours of sleep per night. And as with eating, this is
easier said than done. If you figure it out, let me know what you did. My sleep schedule is
irregular at best.

## 5. Thou shalt not skip cardio

Your aerobic capacity, or [VO2
max](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VO2_max#Clinical_use_to_assess_cardiorespiratory_fitness_and_mortality),
has a strong inverse relationship with all-cause mortality risk. Furthermore, you don't want to end
up muscle-bound and unable to walk up the stairs without taking breaks because your cardiovascular
system is incapable of delivering sufficient oxygen to your body.

Aim for at least two cardio sessions per week. At the beginning, you might go on easy runs or use an
indoor rowing machine. As you progress, you can incorporate high-intensity sessions like sprints
and hill climbs.

# Recommendations

---

## 1. Work with someone who knows what they're doing

Rule #2 gives a checklist for avoiding injury, but following that checklist is easier said than
done. When I was just starting out, I frequently didn't know how different movements were supposed
to feel and couldn't identify when I was making mistakes. So I paid for a personal trainer to teach
me technique and she's been worth her weight in gold. If you can, hire someone to coach you or find
an experienced friend who's willing to teach you.

## 2. Use free weights

If you train at a gym using weights, you'll probably have a choice between machines, like a leg
press, and free weights, like dumbbells and barbells. I recommend avoiding the machines when
possible. Machines isolate individual muscles, which is great for bodybuilders and people training
around injuries (see law #1), but they do so at the expense of training stabilizing muscle groups.
Weak stabilizers are an injury risk (see law #2).

For example, if you need to put a box on a high shelf, you'll appreciate having done overhead press
more than seated machine press. Overhead press trains the smaller stabilizing muscles in your
shoulders and forces your core to engage to keep your body steady under load. Seated machine press
does none of that.

## 3. Movement patterns

It's important to train your whole body. In order of importance, the following are the movement
patterns I've found to be most helpful and examples of exercises that train them.

1. Hinge
    - Deadlift
    - Romanian deadlift
    - Hip thrust
1. Pull
    - Scapular pull-up
    - Pull-up
    - Dumbbell row
    - Dumbbell lat pullover
1. Squat
    - Barbell back squat
    - Barbell front squat
    - Bulgarian split squat
    - Reverse barbell lunge
1. Push
    - Overhead press
    - Dumbbell bench press
1. Carry
    - Farmer's walks
    - Overhead farmer's walks

Whatever you end up doing should incorporate movements from all five categories. Other more focused
exercises, like bicep curls, are also good to do but do not have the same foundational importance
as the big compound movements listed above.
